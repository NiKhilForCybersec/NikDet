The Reality of Vulnerability Management

A Typical Day in Vulnerability Management

Monday morning. You run your weekly vulnerability scan. Results:

VULNERABILITY SCAN SUMMARY
==========================
Total Vulnerabilities: 47,832
  Critical (CVSS 9.0+):  1,247
  High (CVSS 7.0-8.9):   8,934
  Medium (CVSS 4.0-6.9): 24,651
  Low (CVSS 0.1-3.9):    13,000

New since last week: 2,341
Assets scanned: 12,847

Your team capacity: ~200 remediations/week

The math doesn't work: 1,247 criticals. 200 remediations/week. Even if you ONLY worked criticals, it would take 6+ weeks to clear the backlog. And new ones arrive weekly.

This is normal. Every organization faces this. The key is prioritization - not trying to fix everything, but fixing the RIGHT things.

Why CVSS Alone Fails

❌ The CVSS-Only Approach

What happens: "Fix all CVSS 9.0+ vulnerabilities first."

Problem: You end up patching a critical vuln on an isolated test server while ignoring a high-severity vuln on your domain controller.

CVSS Doesn't Tell You:

Missing ContextWhy It MattersExample
Asset importance Vuln on DC vs test box = very different risk CVSS 9.0 on isolated lab server vs CVSS 7.5 on AD server
Exposure Internet-facing vs internal-only Same vuln: public web server vs internal file share
Exploitability Is it actually being exploited in the wild? CVSS 10.0 with no known exploits vs CVSS 7.0 in active campaigns
Compensating controls Other defenses may reduce risk RCE vuln on host with no network access = lower real risk
Data sensitivity What data is at risk? Vuln on PII database vs vuln on public marketing site

Real Example: CVSS Misleads

Vuln A: CVSS 9.8 - RCE in Apache on isolated dev server, no sensitive data, not internet-facing
Vuln B: CVSS 7.5 - SQL injection on customer portal, internet-facing, contains PII, actively exploited in wild

CVSS says: Fix A first (9.8 > 7.5)

Reality says: Fix B first - it's actually exploited, internet-facing, and has customer data

Real-World Prioritization

Risk-Based Prioritization Formula

ACTUAL RISK = CVSS × Asset Value × Exposure × Exploit Availability

Where:
- Asset Value: 1 (low) to 5 (crown jewels)
- Exposure: 1 (isolated) to 5 (internet-facing, no WAF)
- Exploit Availability: 1 (theoretical) to 5 (weaponized, in campaigns)

Example calculations:

Vuln A (Dev Server):
  CVSS 9.8 × Asset(1) × Exposure(1) × Exploit(2) = 19.6

Vuln B (Customer Portal):
  CVSS 7.5 × Asset(4) × Exposure(5) × Exploit(5) = 750

Vuln B priority score is 38x higher despite lower CVSS!

Quick Prioritization Framework

PriorityCriteriaSLA
P1 - Critical Actively exploited + Internet-facing OR on crown jewels 24-48 hours
P2 - High Exploit available + Internet-facing OR CVSS 9+ on important assets 7 days
P3 - Medium CVSS 7+ on internal systems OR CVSS 9+ on low-value assets 30 days
P4 - Low Everything else 90 days or accept risk

CISA KEV - Your Best Friend

✓ Use CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) Catalog

If a vulnerability is in KEV, it's being actively exploited. This immediately makes it P1 regardless of CVSS.

Check: https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog

Prioritization Scenarios

Scenario 1: Zero-Day Announced (Log4Shell style)

Situation: Critical zero-day announced. CVSS 10.0. Exploit code public. You have 500 instances of the vulnerable software.

Wrong approach: "Patch all 500 servers this weekend!"

Problem: You'll burn out the team, break things, and still not finish in time.

Right approach:

  1. Hour 1-4: Identify internet-facing instances (probably 20-30). Patch OR apply WAF rule OR take offline.
  2. Hour 4-8: Patch/mitigate crown jewels (Domain controllers, databases with PII)
  3. Day 2-3: Patch remaining critical systems
  4. Week 1: Patch everything else

Scenario 2: Quarterly Vuln Review Meeting

Situation: CISO asks: "Why do we still have 1,200 critical vulnerabilities?"

Wrong answer: "We're working on it."

Right answer:

"Of the 1,200 criticals:
- 847 are on isolated dev/test systems (accepted risk, documented)
- 203 are pending vendor patches (no fix available yet)
- 89 are scheduled for next maintenance window (7 days)
- 61 are actively being remediated this week

Our internet-facing critical count is 12, down from 34 last quarter.
Zero criticals on crown jewel systems.
Zero vulns from CISA KEV catalog unpatched > 48 hours."

Scenario 3: Application Team Pushes Back

Situation: Critical vuln on production web app. App team says "We can't patch - it'll break the application. Our next release is in 3 months."

Options:

Option 1:WAF rule to block exploit (temporary mitigation)
Option 2:Network segmentation to reduce exposure
Option 3:Enhanced monitoring for exploitation attempts
Option 4:Risk acceptance signed by business owner

Key: Document the decision. If they won't patch AND won't accept compensating controls, escalate to CISO with the risk in business terms.

Organizational Challenges

❌ Challenge 1: "Not My Problem"

Situation: Security identifies vuln. Server team says "We don't own the application." App team says "We don't manage the server."

Reality: 3 months later, vuln still unpatched, everyone pointing fingers.

✓ Solution: Clear Ownership Matrix

  • Define: Who patches OS? Who patches applications?
  • Asset inventory with clear owners
  • Escalation path when owner doesn't respond
  • SLAs tied to performance reviews

❌ Challenge 2: Legacy Systems

Situation: Windows Server 2008 running critical business app. No patches available. Vendor out of business. App team says "It works, don't touch it."

✓ Solution: Compensating Controls

  • Network isolation (own VLAN, strict firewall rules)
  • Enhanced monitoring (every connection logged, alerted)
  • Application whitelisting
  • Documented risk acceptance with sunset date
  • Migrate to supported platform (budget it!)

❌ Challenge 3: Change Freeze

Situation: Critical vuln discovered. But it's Q4 and there's a company-wide change freeze until January.

✓ Solution: Emergency Change Process

  • Define what constitutes "security emergency"
  • Pre-approved process for critical security patches during freeze
  • Requires CISO + IT Director approval
  • Extra testing and rollback plan required
