Security Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about security controls, implementation, and best practices. If you're wondering "why" or "how" about any security topic, start here.

General Security Questions

What's the most important security control to implement first?

Answer: Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

MFA provides the highest security ROI. It stops 99.9% of account compromise attacks with minimal cost and disruption. Without MFA, a single stolen password gives attackers full access. Start with MFA for all users, then expand to other controls.

Priority order: 1) MFA → 2) Email security → 3) EDR → 4) Patching → 5) Backups → 6) Network segmentation

How much should we spend on security?

Industry benchmarks:

  • Average: 5-15% of IT budget
  • Regulated industries (finance, healthcare): 10-20%
  • Tech companies: 15-25%

Better question: What's the cost of a breach vs. the cost of prevention? Average breach costs $4.5M. If $200K/year in security prevents one breach every 10 years, that's $2M spent to prevent $4.5M loss.

Is "good enough" security acceptable?

Answer: It depends on what "good enough" means.

Perfect security doesn't exist. The goal is risk reduction to an acceptable level. What's acceptable depends on:

  • What you're protecting (PII, financial data, IP)
  • Regulatory requirements (you may not have a choice)
  • Threat landscape (are you a high-value target?)
  • Business impact tolerance (what can you survive?)

However: Basic hygiene (MFA, patching, backups, EDR) is non-negotiable. Skipping these is negligence, not risk acceptance.

What's the difference between a vulnerability and a risk?

Vulnerability: A weakness that could be exploited. Example: Unpatched Apache server with known CVE.

Threat: Something that might exploit a vulnerability. Example: Attackers actively scanning for vulnerable Apache servers.

Risk: The likelihood and impact of a threat exploiting a vulnerability. Example: 70% chance of breach within 90 days, $500K potential loss.

Formula: Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Impact

You fix vulnerabilities, defend against threats, and manage risks.

Identity & Access Questions

Why can't I just use strong passwords instead of MFA?

Because passwords fail in multiple ways that MFA prevents:

  • Phishing: Users enter strong passwords into fake login pages. MFA (especially FIDO2) won't work on fake sites.
  • Credential stuffing: Passwords from breached sites are tried everywhere. MFA blocks these attempts.
  • Keyloggers: Malware captures passwords but can't capture time-based MFA codes.
  • Password reuse: Users reuse passwords despite policies. MFA protects even if password is reused and leaked elsewhere.

Strong passwords are still important, but they're only one factor. MFA adds something you HAVE or ARE to something you KNOW.

What's the difference between SSO and MFA?

SSO (Single Sign-On): Sign in once, access many applications. Convenience feature that reduces password fatigue.

MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication): Require two or more factors to prove identity. Security feature that prevents account takeover.

They complement each other:

  • SSO without MFA: Convenient but risky (one password = access to everything)
  • MFA without SSO: Secure but annoying (MFA prompt for every app)
  • SSO + MFA: Best of both (one MFA-protected login, access to all apps)
Should executives have different security requirements?

Yes, but STRICTER, not looser.

Executives are high-value targets for:

  • BEC (Business Email Compromise) - impersonating execs for wire fraud
  • Whaling - targeted phishing of executives
  • Credential theft - their access is more valuable

Executives should have:

  • Phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2 keys)
  • Enhanced monitoring on their accounts
  • More restricted mobile device policies
  • Personal security awareness training

Never: Exempt executives from security controls because they're "too busy" or "important."

What's "Privileged Access Management" and do I need it?

PAM protects admin accounts - the keys to your kingdom.

Admin accounts (Domain Admin, Global Admin, root) can:

  • Access any data
  • Create/delete users
  • Disable security controls
  • Deploy ransomware company-wide

PAM provides:

  • Vaulting: Passwords stored securely, not known to admins
  • Just-in-time access: Admin rights granted only when needed, auto-expire
  • Session recording: Record what admins do with elevated access
  • Approval workflows: Require approval for sensitive access

You need PAM if: You have more than a handful of admins, or any regulatory requirements (SOX, PCI, HIPAA).

Data Protection Questions

If everything is encrypted, why do I need DLP?

Encryption and DLP solve different problems:

Encryption: Protects data from unauthorized access if storage/transmission is compromised.

DLP: Prevents authorized users from sending data to unauthorized places.

Example: An employee has legitimate access to customer data (they can decrypt it). They email it to their personal account to take to a competitor. Encryption didn't stop this - they had the key. DLP would block or alert on the transfer.

Encryption protects against outsiders. DLP protects against insiders and accidents.

What data should be classified as "Confidential" vs "Restricted"?

Think about impact of exposure:

Restricted (highest): Exposure causes severe harm

  • Credentials, passwords, API keys
  • Customer payment data (PCI)
  • SSNs, government IDs
  • Trade secrets, M&A data
  • Health records (PHI)

Confidential: Exposure causes significant harm

  • Financial reports (pre-release)
  • Employee PII (non-SSN)
  • Customer lists, pricing
  • Contracts, legal documents
  • Internal strategies

Rule of thumb: If it would be a news headline or trigger breach notification, it's at least Confidential. If it's regulated data or would cause existential business harm, it's Restricted.

Is cloud storage secure? Should I trust Microsoft/Google with my data?

Cloud providers are generally more secure than on-premises for most organizations.

Why cloud is often more secure:

  • 24/7 security teams larger than most companies
  • Physical security at data centers
  • Automatic patching and updates
  • Encryption by default
  • Compliance certifications (SOC 2, ISO 27001, etc.)

Your responsibilities:

  • Proper configuration (misconfig is #1 cloud breach cause)
  • Access control (who can access your tenant)
  • Data classification and handling
  • Monitoring for suspicious activity

If you need more control: Use Customer-Managed Keys (CMK) so Microsoft/Google can't access your data even if compelled.

Endpoint Security Questions

What's the difference between antivirus and EDR?

Antivirus (AV): Traditional, signature-based. Knows about malware X, blocks malware X.

  • Matches files against database of known bad
  • Can't detect new/unknown malware
  • Limited visibility into what malware did

EDR (Endpoint Detection & Response): Behavior-based, comprehensive visibility.

  • Monitors process behavior, not just file signatures
  • Detects "living off the land" attacks using legitimate tools
  • Records full timeline of activity for investigation
  • Enables response: isolate machine, kill process, collect evidence

Analogy: AV is like a "wanted poster" that catches known criminals. EDR is like a security camera system that catches suspicious behavior and records everything.

Should I allow personal devices (BYOD)?

It's a risk/convenience tradeoff with no universal answer.

Arguments for BYOD:

  • Employee satisfaction, flexibility
  • Cost savings (no device purchase)
  • Users prefer their own devices
  • Can't stop it entirely anyway

Arguments against BYOD:

  • Can't fully manage/secure
  • Data exposure risk
  • Support complexity
  • Compliance challenges

If you allow BYOD:

  • Require device registration and compliance (MDM)
  • Use app-level protection (MAM) to contain corporate data
  • Limit what data can be accessed from BYOD
  • Enable remote wipe of corporate data
How quickly do I really need to patch?

Depends on the vulnerability, but faster than you think.

Timeline reality:

  • Vulnerability disclosed → Exploits available: Often within hours/days
  • Actively exploited vulnerabilities: Attackers already using them
  • Average time to weaponize: 7-14 days for critical CVEs

Recommended SLAs:

  • Critical + Actively exploited: 24-72 hours
  • Critical: 7 days
  • High: 14 days
  • Medium: 30 days
  • Low: 90 days

Reality check: If you can't patch quickly, have compensating controls (network isolation, additional monitoring, WAF rules).

Network Security Questions

Do I still need a firewall if I'm in the cloud?

Yes, but it looks different.

Traditional firewall role: Control traffic at network perimeter.

In cloud, you need:

  • Cloud-native firewalls: Security Groups (AWS), NSGs (Azure), firewall rules (GCP)
  • Web Application Firewalls (WAF): Protect web apps from attacks
  • Cloud firewalls: Azure Firewall, AWS Network Firewall for centralized control
  • SaaS security: CASB for SaaS traffic visibility

The perimeter moved, but the need for traffic control didn't disappear.

What is Zero Trust and do I need it?

Zero Trust is a security model, not a product.

Core principle: Never trust, always verify. Don't assume internal = safe.

Key components:

  • Verify explicitly: Authenticate and authorize every access request based on all available data (identity, device, location, etc.)
  • Least privilege: Give minimum access needed, just-in-time
  • Assume breach: Design as if attackers are already inside

Do you need it? You should adopt Zero Trust principles. Full implementation takes time - it's a journey, not a destination. Start with:

  • MFA everywhere
  • Device compliance checks
  • Network segmentation
  • Least privilege access
Should I inspect encrypted (HTTPS) traffic?

It's a tradeoff between security visibility and privacy/complexity.

Arguments for TLS inspection:

  • 90%+ of web traffic is encrypted - without inspection, you're blind
  • Malware uses HTTPS to hide C2 traffic
  • DLP can't see encrypted content

Arguments against:

  • Privacy concerns (you can see everything)
  • Breaks certificate pinning in some apps
  • Compliance issues in some industries
  • Performance overhead

Best practice: Inspect with exceptions for banking, healthcare, and other sensitive sites. Be transparent with employees.

Monitoring & Detection Questions

Do I need a SOC? Can't the tools just alert me?

Tools generate alerts. Humans analyze and respond.

What happens without a SOC:

  • Alerts pile up unreviewed
  • False positives overwhelm true positives
  • No one to investigate when alerts fire at 2 AM
  • Attacks go unnoticed for months

SOC options:

  • In-house SOC: Full control, expensive (24/7 needs ~8-10 analysts minimum)
  • Managed SOC (MSSP/MDR): External team monitors, you respond to escalations. More affordable.
  • Hybrid: In-house during business hours, MSSP after hours

Minimum: Someone must review alerts daily. If you can't staff it, outsource it.

How do I know if I've been breached?

Often you don't - until it's obvious.

Signs of compromise:

  • Unusual login locations or times
  • New admin accounts you didn't create
  • Unexpected software or services running
  • High outbound data transfer
  • Disabled security tools
  • Customer reports of spam from your domain
  • Ransomware note (obvious, but too late)

Proactive detection:

  • Regular threat hunting
  • SIEM with behavioral analytics
  • Compromise assessments
  • External attack surface monitoring
  • Dark web monitoring for leaked credentials
How long should I keep security logs?

Long enough to investigate incidents, meet compliance, and support legal needs.

Minimum recommendations:

  • Security alerts/incidents: 7 years (legal)
  • Authentication logs: 1-2 years
  • Firewall/network logs: 90 days hot, 1 year archive
  • Endpoint telemetry: 30-90 days hot
  • Email logs: 90 days (longer for compliance)

Regulatory requirements vary:

  • PCI-DSS: 1 year, 3 months immediately available
  • HIPAA: 6 years
  • SOX: 7 years
  • GDPR: As long as necessary (minimize)
