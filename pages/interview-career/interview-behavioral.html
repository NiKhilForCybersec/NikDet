Foundation Questions

Q: Tell me about yourself 90-120 sec

I have about 5 years in cybersecurity, with around 3 years focused on security engineering — SIEM and endpoint security specifically.

I started as a SOC analyst at Cognizant, working with Splunk on alert triage and incident investigations. That gave me a strong foundation in understanding how security operations actually function day-to-day.

I then moved into security engineering at Infosys, working with enterprise platforms like Trellix and Symantec DLP. I was involved in a major EDR evaluation project where I helped compare Trellix against Cortex XDR and presented recommendations to stakeholders.

After relocating to Canada, I joined a Siemens subsidiary — a B2B SaaS analytics platform serving Fortune 500 clients. I handled the MDE deployment across 4,500 endpoints and worked on multiple initiatives in the RSA NetWitness to Microsoft Sentinel migration — log source onboarding, detection translation, and operational readiness. I also contributed to the DLP transition from Symantec to Purview.

What excites me about this role is applying what I've done internally in a consulting capacity — assessing client environments, designing SOC modernization roadmaps, and delivering measurable outcomes.

Structure: Foundation → Growth → Impact → Why Consulting

Q: Why consulting? Why not stay in-house? 60 sec

I'll be honest — in-house engineering roles, once processes stabilize, become repetitive. You're working the same tickets every day. Exclusion requests. Agent not reporting. Policy exceptions.

Don't get me wrong — I enjoyed building it. The transformation work was challenging. But once the machine is running, you're maintaining it, not building anymore.

What I realized is that I enjoy the problem-solving at the start of projects — assessing current state, identifying gaps, designing the approach. That's the work that energizes me. In consulting, that's the core of what you do.

I'll learn faster seeing 10 different security programs than staying in one for 10 years.

Avoid: Sounding negative about current role or saying "I'm bored"

Leadership & Influence

Q: Tell me about a time you had to influence without authority 90 sec

SITUATION We discovered that multiple teams were creating Azure Storage blobs for static websites without HTTPS — data exposed to the public, unencrypted. My manager asked me to figure out a process to find and track these.

TASK His initial suggestion was to develop a Python or PowerShell script to scan and identify these misconfigurations. It was a reasonable approach, but I thought there might be a better way.

ACTION I realized we already had diagnostic logs and Azure Activity logs enabled. Instead of building a separate scanning tool, we could write a KQL query to identify exposed storage accounts and track them in a Sentinel workbook.

I pitched this to my manager: "Instead of a script that runs periodically, we get real-time visibility. The workbook shows current state, tracks remediation progress, and we can set up alerts for new violations."

RESULT My manager accepted the approach as a better solution. We implemented the workbook, and now we have continuous visibility into storage security posture rather than point-in-time scans.

Key message: "I didn't just disagree — I offered a solution that solved the underlying problem better"

Q: Tell me about a time you resolved a conflict between teams 60 sec

SITUATION During the MDE deployment, IT Ops had created a golden image with the MDE agent pre-installed. When they deployed it, 50+ devices ended up with duplicate device IDs — they couldn't be uniquely identified in the console.

CONFLICT IT Ops wanted to re-image all affected machines. That would take weeks and disrupt users. I knew there was a better way — offboard, wait for the cleanup period, re-onboard with fresh IDs. But I had no authority over their team.

ACTION Instead of arguing, I offered to prove it. I took 5 pilot machines, ran the offboarding script, let them sit for the required period, then re-onboarded. Each got a clean, unique device ID. I documented the process and helped them update the golden image SOP.

RESULT We fixed all 50 devices without re-imaging. IT Ops started coming to me for advice on other MDE issues because I'd proven I was there to help, not criticize.

Key message: "I influenced by demonstrating, not by demanding"

Challenges & Failures

Q: Tell me about your biggest failure 60-90 sec

SITUATION During MDE rollout, I enabled an ASR rule that blocks unsigned PowerShell scripts. I tested it in audit mode for 2 weeks — no alerts, looked clean. Felt confident we'd caught everything.

WHAT WENT WRONG Two days after enabling block mode, Finance escalated — their month-end reporting scripts were being blocked. These scripts only run monthly, so my 2-week audit window completely missed them.

IMPACT Finance couldn't run their month-end reports. I had to create an emergency exclusion while we fixed it properly.

WHAT I LEARNED Audit windows need to cover full business cycles, not arbitrary time periods. Now I run audit mode for a full month minimum for any blocking rule, and I specifically ask stakeholders about scheduled tasks.

PROCESS IMPROVEMENT I created a pre-enforcement checklist that includes confirming with business units about periodic scripts. That checklist is now part of our standard ASR deployment process.

Key elements: Own the failure, explain what you learned, show systemic fix

Q: Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news 60 sec

SITUATION At a previous role, we had FireEye HX with URL inspection enabled. Users at a subsidiary started reporting that their HR portal wasn't loading properly.

INVESTIGATION The HR portal used JavaScript components that FireEye's URL inspection was blocking. I opened a support case with FireEye — they confirmed it was a known limitation with no fix available.

THE BAD NEWS I had to tell leadership: "There's no fix. We have to choose between full security coverage or a working HR portal."

ACTION I didn't just bring the problem — I brought options. Option A: Keep it broken (unacceptable). Option B: Create a separate device group for those HR systems and apply a profile with URL inspection turned off, while documenting the accepted risk.

RESULT Leadership accepted Option B with the documented risk. Months later a vendor update fixed the issue and we re-enabled full inspection.

Key message: "I don't just deliver bad news — I bring solutions"

Communication Skills

Q: Tell me about explaining something technical to a non-technical audience 60-90 sec

SITUATION During our MDE rollout, I noticed the VP of Sales' laptop hadn't checked in for about 5 days. Senior managers were on our priority list for security monitoring.

ACTION I emailed him asking to schedule 15 minutes. He replied wanting to know what's actually happening first.

On the call, I kept it simple: "MDE is our endpoint security tool that detects malware and threats. Your laptop stopped reporting — probably a Windows update broke something. I need 15 minutes to push a fresh agent. Won't affect your work."

No jargon. No "sensor telemetry connectivity issues." Just: "Security tool stopped working, I'll fix it, takes 15 minutes."

RESULT He said "Got it, let's do it tomorrow." Fixed the corrupted service, pushed agent via Intune, confirmed reporting. He appreciated that I explained what the tool actually does instead of just fixing it silently.

Shows: Can translate tech to business, respects executive time, proactive communication

Growth & Initiative

Q: Describe a time you had to learn something quickly 60 sec

SITUATION I'm co-founding DietIQ, a health-tech startup. When I joined, they were planning to release the app with a UI that honestly wasn't impressive. I told them — your product can have great features, but if the UI/UX isn't engaging, users won't stick around.

ACTION I had UI/UX experience from college, but the tools had changed tremendously. So I took the initiative. I learned Figma's new features — auto-layout, components, prototyping, design systems. Then I worked for about 40 hours straight with minimal breaks and designed the entire mobile app UI/UX from scratch.

RESULT That UI is still being used in production today. The app has grown, they've added features on top of it, and now we're planning a UI/UX refresh for the B2B healthcare features.

Shows: Rapid learning, initiative beyond job scope, delivery under pressure

Q: Tell me about a time you went above and beyond 60 sec

SITUATION At a previous role, we had FireEye HX deployed across the environment. Compliance checks were completely manual — every week, someone had to log into multiple HX consoles, download device lists, merge them in Excel, compare against the asset inventory, and follow up on non-compliant devices.

PROBLEM This took 4-5 hours every week. It was error-prone. And honestly, it was the kind of tedious work that nobody wanted to do.

ACTION I proposed automating the entire process. The script pulled device lists from all HX consoles via API, merged them, compared against our CMDB, generated a compliance report, and flagged devices that needed attention.

RESULT What used to take 4-5 hours of manual work every week now ran automatically in minutes. We went from weekly spot-checks to daily compliance visibility.

Key message: "I don't just do what's assigned — I look for ways to make things better"

Working with Difficult People

Q: Tell me about working with a difficult person 60 sec

SITUATION During the MDE rollout, the IT Ops lead pushed back hard on installing MDE on his systems. Many of the servers he managed were legacy — older hardware, a bit slow. ESET alone was already causing performance issues on some of them.

His concern was valid: "If I add another security agent on top of ESET, these systems will crawl." He wasn't being difficult — he was protecting his environment.

ACTION Instead of forcing the issue, I worked with him. First, I moved his systems to the last deployment waves — we'd stabilize XDR on newer systems first. That gave him time and gave me data.

When it was his turn, I came with evidence: "MDE uses 1-5% CPU in steady state. And we're not adding it on top of ESET — we're replacing ESET. You'll actually have fewer agents, not more."

RESULT He agreed to a pilot on 5 machines. Performance was fine. We rolled out to the rest without issues.

Key message: "Difficult people" often have valid concerns you haven't addressed

Consulting-Specific Scenarios

Q: How would you handle a client who disagrees with your recommendation? 60 sec

First, I'd make sure I understand why they disagree. Is it technical concerns? Budget? Politics? Timeline? The surface objection often isn't the real issue.

If it's a legitimate difference of opinion, I'd present the tradeoffs clearly. "Option A has these benefits and these risks. Option B has these. Here's my recommendation and here's why, but here's what you'd need to mitigate if you go the other way."

Ultimately, it's their organization. My job is to give them the best advice and the information to make a good decision. Document the recommendation, document their decision, move forward as a partner.

The one exception: if it's a security decision that creates unacceptable risk — like disabling MFA to make logins faster — I'd escalate within our firm before proceeding.

Shows: Client-centric thinking, professional judgment, knowing when to escalate

Q: What if you realize you don't know how to solve the problem? 45 sec

Consulting is a team sport. First, I'd leverage the firm's network — who's done this before? What knowledge bases exist? What past engagements are relevant?

I'd be transparent with the engagement manager: "Here's what I know, here's where I need support." Better to ask early than struggle silently.

With the client, I'd manage expectations without undermining confidence. "Let me research this and get back to you by end of day" is better than guessing.

The ability to quickly learn and find answers is more valuable than already knowing everything.

General Questions Quick Reference

Q: What's your greatest strength? 45 sec

Two things: communication and problem-solving approach.

On communication — I've gotten much better at explaining technical issues clearly to different audiences. Whether it's a quick Slack message to a colleague or walking a VP through why their laptop needs attention, I can adjust how I explain things.

On problem-solving — instead of just patching issues to make them go away, I try to find the root cause. And then I document it. So when the same problem comes up again, we're not starting from scratch. "Fix it, understand why, write it down."

Q: What's your greatest weakness? 45 sec

Early in my career, I preferred to work alone. If I was stuck on something, I'd spend hours trying to figure it out myself instead of asking for help. I was worried people would judge my knowledge.

Experience taught me that's not how it works. Nobody judges you for asking questions — they actually appreciate it because it means problems get solved faster.

Now if I'm stuck on something for 30 minutes to an hour and not making progress, I reach out. "Here's what I'm seeing, here's what I've tried, what am I missing?"

Good weakness formula: Real weakness + How you learned + Concrete change in behavior

Q: Where do you see yourself in 5 years? 45 sec

Leading security transformation engagements — the full lifecycle from assessment to implementation. I want to be the person clients trust to come in, understand their environment, and design a roadmap that actually works.

In the near term, that means building expertise across different industries and security domains. Learning what works in healthcare vs. financial services vs. manufacturing.

Longer term, I see myself mentoring junior consultants, bringing in new business, and shaping how the practice approaches emerging areas — AI security, cloud-native architectures, XDR strategies.
