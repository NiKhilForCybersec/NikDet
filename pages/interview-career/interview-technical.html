SIEM & Detection Architecture

Q: What's your view on SIEM vs. XDR? 60 sec

They're complementary, not competing. XDR is depth — deep visibility and correlation within its ecosystem. Microsoft Defender XDR correlates endpoint, identity, email, and cloud app signals better than any SIEM rule could.

SIEM is breadth — it aggregates across ecosystems. Your Palo Alto firewall logs, your custom app logs, your third-party SaaS audit logs — XDR doesn't see those.

In our transformation, we explicitly designed for this. XDR handles what it's good at — endpoint threats, identity attacks, email compromise chains. SIEM handles cross-platform correlation, long-term hunting, compliance retention, and anything XDR can't see.

The mistake is trying to recreate XDR detections in SIEM. We retired 780 rules that were doing exactly that — poorly duplicating what Defender already does natively with better context.

Q: Walk me through your SIEM migration approach 90 sec

Four phases over 12 months:

Phase 1 - Discovery (Months 1-2): Inventory everything. 47 log sources in RSA, 1,200+ detection rules, current alert volumes, SOC workflows. Also assessed what we were missing — cloud-native sources RSA couldn't handle well.

Phase 2 - Architecture (Months 2-4): Designed the Sentinel workspace structure, data collection strategy, cost model. Key decision: single workspace vs. multiple. We went single with RBAC for multi-tenant visibility while controlling access.

Phase 3 - Parallel Operation (Months 4-10): Both SIEMs running simultaneously. Migrated log sources in waves — started with cloud-native (Entra ID, M365) where Sentinel has native connectors, then moved to on-prem sources. Built detection rules in parallel, validated coverage.

Phase 4 - Cutover (Months 10-12): SOC fully on Sentinel. RSA in read-only for historical queries. Final decommission after 90-day confidence period.

Critical success factor: we didn't just migrate rules — we rationalized. 1,200 rules became 485 because we leveraged XDR and eliminated redundancy.

Q: How do you approach detection engineering? 60-90 sec

I use a threat-informed approach, not a log-driven approach.

Start with threats, not data: What attacks matter to this organization? For us, it was data exfiltration (we process customer CRM data), identity compromise (privileged access to customer tenants), and supply chain (API integrations with Salesforce).

Map to MITRE ATT&CK: For each priority threat, identify the techniques. Data exfiltration? That's T1041 (Exfiltration Over C2), T1567 (Exfiltration to Cloud Storage), T1048 (Exfiltration Over Alternative Protocol).

Assess data availability: Do we have visibility into these techniques? What log sources? What fields? Sometimes the answer is "we can't detect this" — that's a control gap to address.

Build layered detections: Not one rule per technique — multiple detection points. Behavioral baselines, threshold alerts, correlation rules. Defense in depth.

Validate and tune: Every rule gets tested with realistic scenarios. False positive rate tracked. Detection logic refined based on real alerts.

SOC Maturity Assessments

Q: How would you approach a SOC maturity assessment for a new client? 90 sec

I'd use a structured framework — SOC-CMM is my go-to — but customize it to the client's context.

Step 1 - Stakeholder interviews: Not just the SOC manager. Talk to analysts, IT ops, incident responders, and business stakeholders. Understand pain points from multiple perspectives.

Step 2 - Documentation review: Runbooks, escalation procedures, SLAs, metrics they're tracking. The gap between documented process and actual practice tells you a lot.

Step 3 - Technical assessment: Tool coverage, log source inventory, detection rule quality, automation level. I'd sample some alerts and trace them through the full lifecycle.

Step 4 - Metrics analysis: MTTD, MTTR, false positive rates, alert-to-incident ratios. But also qualitative — how stressed is the team? What's the turnover rate?

Step 5 - Gap analysis and roadmap: Map current state to desired state. Prioritize recommendations by impact and feasibility. Quick wins vs. strategic investments.

Key principle: maturity targets should match organizational context. A 200-person startup doesn't need the same SOC as a Fortune 500.

Q: How do you measure security program effectiveness? 60 sec

Three categories of metrics:

Operational metrics — the basics. MTTD, MTTR, alert volume, false positive rate, SLA compliance. These tell you if the machine is running.

Coverage metrics — are we actually protected? MITRE ATT&CK coverage percentage, log source coverage, endpoint protection coverage. We built a heat map showing which techniques we can detect vs. blind spots.

Business-aligned metrics — the ones leadership cares about. Cost per alert, cost avoidance from prevented incidents, compliance audit findings, time to onboard new log sources. These connect security to business value.

The trap is measuring what's easy instead of what matters. Alert count is easy. "Did we detect the threats that matter to our business?" is harder but more valuable.

MITRE ATT&CK & Threat Frameworks

Q: How do you use MITRE ATT&CK in practice? 60 sec

Three primary use cases:

1. Detection coverage mapping: Every detection rule we build maps to ATT&CK techniques. We maintain a coverage heat map — green where we have strong detection, red for gaps. This drives prioritization.

2. Threat intelligence integration: When we get intel on threat actors targeting our industry, we map their TTPs to ATT&CK. "APT29 uses T1566 for initial access, T1053 for persistence." Then we verify our detection coverage for those specific techniques.

3. Incident analysis: After any incident, we map the attack chain to ATT&CK. This identifies where detection failed, where we got lucky, and where to improve.

The framework isn't just for reporting — it's an operational tool for prioritizing security investments.

Q: Walk me through your detection coverage for T1078 - Valid Accounts 90 sec

This is a high-priority technique for us because we're a SaaS company with privileged access to customer data.

Layer 1 - Entra ID native detections: Impossible travel, unfamiliar sign-in properties, password spray detection. These are Microsoft's ML-based detections — we ingest the alerts into Sentinel.

Layer 2 - Behavioral baselines: Custom KQL rules that baseline normal login patterns per user. Alert on deviations — new device, new location, unusual time.

Layer 3 - Correlation rules: Combine identity signals with other data. "User logged in from new IP AND accessed sensitive SharePoint site AND downloaded unusual volume of files" — that's a correlation Entra ID alone can't do.

Layer 4 - Privileged account monitoring: Stricter rules for admin accounts. Any interactive login outside of break-glass scenarios generates an alert.

Data sources: Entra ID sign-in logs, audit logs, Conditional Access logs, Defender for Cloud Apps activity.

Q: What experience do you have with KQL? Show me an example. 60 sec

I write KQL daily for detection rules, hunting queries, and incident investigation. Here's a real example — detecting potential data exfiltration via SharePoint:

// Detect unusual SharePoint download volume
let threshold = 100; // files
let timeWindow = 1h;
let userBaseline = OfficeActivity
| where TimeGenerated between (ago(30d) .. ago(1d))
| where Operation == "FileDownloaded"
| summarize AvgDailyDownloads = count() / 30 by UserId;

OfficeActivity
| where TimeGenerated > ago(timeWindow)
| where Operation == "FileDownloaded"
| summarize
    DownloadCount = count(),
    Files = make_set(OfficeObjectId, 10),
    SourceIPs = make_set(ClientIP)
    by UserId
| join kind=leftouter userBaseline on UserId
| where DownloadCount > threshold
    or DownloadCount > (AvgDailyDownloads * 5)
| extend Anomaly = iff(DownloadCount > (AvgDailyDownloads * 5),
    "Baseline Deviation", "Threshold Exceeded")

This combines threshold-based detection with behavioral baselining. Pure threshold misses low-and-slow exfiltration. Pure baseline misses new users. Combining both gives better coverage.

Common Technical Questions

Q: Walk me through how you'd investigate a potential breach 90 sec

Step 1 - Triage and scope: What triggered this? Alert, user report, external notification? Determine if it's a true positive before full mobilization.

Step 2 - Containment assessment: Is this ongoing? Do we need to isolate systems immediately? Balance between stopping the attack and preserving evidence.

Step 3 - Evidence collection: Preserve logs, memory dumps if needed, timeline of events. In Sentinel, I'd pull all related alerts, hunt for the user/device across all log sources.

Step 4 - Scope determination: Is this one device or lateral movement? Check for related IOCs across the environment. In MDE, use the device timeline and advanced hunting to trace the attack chain.

Step 5 - Root cause: How did they get in? Phishing, credential compromise, vulnerability? This determines remediation.

Step 6 - Remediation and recovery: Remove the threat, patch the vulnerability, reset credentials, restore from clean backups if needed.

Step 7 - Post-incident: Document everything, lessons learned, detection improvements. Did our alerts fire? If not, why not?

Q: Explain the difference between EDR and XDR 45 sec

EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) is focused on endpoints — workstations, servers. It collects telemetry from the endpoint, detects threats, and enables response actions like isolation or remediation. MDE, CrowdStrike, SentinelOne — these are EDR tools.

XDR (Extended Detection and Response) extends that correlation across multiple security domains — endpoint, identity, email, cloud apps, network. Instead of investigating in silos, you get a unified view.

The practical difference: with EDR alone, if an attacker phishes a user and then moves laterally, you might see the endpoint alerts but miss the email context. With XDR, Defender correlates the phishing email, the credential compromise in Entra ID, and the lateral movement on endpoints into one incident.

Q: How do you handle false positives? 45 sec

Track metrics: We measure false positive rate per detection rule. If a rule has >50% FP rate over 30 days, it goes into review.

Tune, don't disable: First option is tuning — add exclusions for known good behavior, tighten the detection logic, add correlation conditions. Disabling is last resort.

Root cause analysis: Why is this firing falsely? Is the detection logic too broad? Is there a legitimate business process we didn't account for?

Feedback loop: Analysts flag FPs during triage. That feedback drives tuning. We review top FP generators weekly.

The goal isn't zero false positives — that usually means you're missing things. The goal is a manageable rate where analysts trust the alerts.

Q: SPL vs KQL — what's different? 45 sec

Both are powerful query languages, but different philosophies.

SPL (Splunk): Pipe-based, left to right. Very flexible, almost like a scripting language. Great for complex transformations. Can be harder to read when queries get long.

KQL (Kusto): Also pipe-based, but more structured. Strong typing. Better for columnar data. Some operations like joins are more explicit.

Practical differences:

  • SPL uses stats, KQL uses summarize
  • SPL uses eval for calculations, KQL uses extend
  • KQL let statements are cleaner than SPL subsearches

I'm fluent in both. The concepts transfer — aggregations, joins, time-based analysis. Learning the second language after the first is mostly syntax.
